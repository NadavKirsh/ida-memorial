Ida (Yittale) Kafka

1924 — 2009

"To not mention her parents' names—Chaya and Eli Boruch Rakowsky—would be to delete the very essence of my Mother's being. And oh, what a being she was."

February 9, 1924

Childhood in Bendein

Ida was born overlooking an old castle and the Przemsha River. The family lived in a single large room serving as kitchen, bedroom, and living space. Despite modest means, the home overflowed with "Yeddishkite" and warmth.

Friday nights were sacred. Before Shabbat, Ida and her friend Salla would walk designated streets collecting challah for the needy in a white pillowcase. As she entered those homes, she felt the air take on a "heavenly peace," leaving the hardships of the week behind.

Historical Context

Pre-war Bedzin (Bendein) was a thriving hub of Jewish culture in Poland. Jews made up nearly half the town's population, creating a deeply connected, charitable, and spiritually rich community despite widespread economic struggles.

The Profession

With rising anti-Semitism and economic boycotts in the late 1930s, securing a physical trade was seen as a vital lifeline. Vocational training offered a semblance of security against an unknown future.

1931 — 1939

A Formative Loss

Tragedy struck early. In 1931, an epidemic swept the area, and her beloved father, Eli Boruch, died of typhus. To survive, her mother Chaya began laboring over custom-made shirts.

Knowing the precariousness of their situation, her mother sent a young Ida to travel 8km daily to Sosnowiec to learn dressmaking. "You should never need it, but you should have a profession in your hands," her mother warned. Ida graduated with honors just as rumors of war began to cross the border.

November 1939

The Flames of Bendein

The vibrant city became a ghost town as German soldiers filled the streets. Watching from an attic window, Ida and her family saw great plumes of smoke rising from their magnificent "Great Shul." It was the moment they realized their entire world was ending. Forced from their home, the Jews of Bendein became the sole targets of the invaders.

World Timeline

September 1, 1939: Germany invades Poland. By September 8, the SS arrived in Bedzin and systematically burned the Great Synagogue along with the surrounding Jewish homes, shooting those who tried to escape the flames.

The Schmelt Camps

Young Jews were funneled into a network of forced labor camps managed by Organization Schmelt, deeply integrated into the German war economy, producing textiles and munitions.

February 1941

Separation & Sagan

Shortly after her 17th birthday, Ida was "mercilessly torn away" from her family. At the orphanage holding center, her brave younger sister Rivkale managed to walk right beside her past the armed guards to deliver their mother's last message: "Take care, and G-d willing, we will see each other soon."

Sent to the *Sagan Tuch Fabrik*, she surrendered her belongings but dared to hide family photos. Staring at a bowl of watery soup she couldn't bring herself to eat, she met Masha Haida. They exchanged glances across the table, realized they were from the same town, and became best friends.

1941 — 1944

Defiance in Greenberg

Ida was transferred to *Greenberg Shlesien*. Assigned to the "Ausneherai," she did "invisible mending" on fabrics. Her German instructor walked in on the first day, looked at the girls, and asked in shock, "Where are your horns?" having been taught that Jews were literal demons.

Despite the terror, acts of spiritual defiance remained. Around Chanukah, Ida and two friends from Bais Yaakov dared to ask a guard to light a candle. Miraculously permitted, Ida, known for her beautiful voice, lit the light and sang "Maoz Tzur." In the middle of total darkness, they injected a ray of hope.

Spiritual Resistance

Maintaining religious observance in the camps was strictly forbidden and punishable by death. Acts like singing prayers or lighting makeshift candles were profound forms of spiritual resistance to maintain humanity.

Dehumanization

"Selections" were designed not only to weed out the weak for extermination but to strip prisoners of all dignity. Those showing signs of illness, like Tuberculosis, disappeared forever.

1944

The Darkest Days

The Germans began "experiments" and brutal inspections. Ida vividly remembered the terror of being forced to walk completely naked down a hall toward three seated SS men. "I tried to cover myself with my hands, it was 'ferboten' (forbidden)."

Girls began deteriorating. Her friend Estusia contracted TB and disappeared. When Ida's eyesight failed at the massive spinning machines, she was denied glasses. But in an open miracle, a sympathetic guard transferred her to the kitchen, likely saving her from starving before the final march.

January — April 1945

The Death March

In the freezing winter, they were ordered to march. Walking 25-30km daily, girls who stopped were shot in the woods. When Ida's feet swelled so badly her shoes fell apart, she bound them in sackcloth and string.

One morning, suffering from severe dysentery in a warehouse in the rain, a wise friend commanded her to urinate in a can and drink it. Shocked, but desperate to live, she obeyed—and the illness passed. It was a brutal, miraculous survival.

The Final Evacuations

As Soviet forces closed in from the East, the SS forced hundreds of thousands of prisoners on foot toward the interior of the Reich. Exposure, starvation, and summary executions killed tens of thousands.

The Collapse

By May 1945, the Nazi command structure was disintegrating. Guards often abandoned their posts and prisoners as Allied forces surrounded them.

May 5-6, 1945

Silence in the Forest

Exhausted, pouring rain, the small remaining group was ordered to sit in a pitch-black forest on a mountain. Ida heard the guards calling to each other—"Frans, Fritz"—and saw flashlights moving away. Then, total silence.

Waking the next morning, the guards were gone. "I am a free human being. I am free!" she wrote. They walked down to the Czech village of Prachatice. The villagers, horrified at the sight of these walking skeletons, cried "Nemecka Cultura" (German Culture) and immediately turned a school into a hospital to save them.

July 1945

The Empty Home

After four years of torture, Ida made the journey back to Bendein. But the homecoming was a nightmare. The streets felt dull; the windows where her sisters used to sit were empty.

Standing in front of her home's door, the pressure became too great. "The dramatic force of my indescribable pain broke... I realize that I am all alone, like a stone, and with a stone in my heart." The mother she had promised to return to was gone.

The Void

For many survivors, liberation did not bring immediate joy, but rather the devastating realization of total annihilation. Returning to Poland often meant facing hostile locals and the undeniable truth that their families had been murdered.

Displaced Persons

Camps like Feldafing in Germany became massive hubs for survivors waiting to emigrate. In these camps, thousands of marriages took place as young people desperately sought to recreate the families they had lost.

1945 — 1946

New Roots in Munich

Refusing to be broken, Ida moved to the Displaced Persons camps in Germany (Feldafing and Regensburg). There, she met Eliezar Kafka. Both were alone; both were determined to rebuild the Jewish people.

They married on July 27, 1946, in a simple ceremony with no special clothes. Shortly after, they welcomed their daughter Helen. Given the starvation and physical abuse Ida had endured, giving birth to a healthy baby was deemed a literal miracle.

1948 & Beyond

Across the Ocean

Securing visas to Canada, they faced one last hurdle: infants under six months weren't allowed on the ships. They traded a watch to an official to falsify Helen's papers. After a terrifying 10-day voyage on a wooden boat, they landed in Halifax.

There, an immigration officer anglicized "Yittale" to "Ida." They settled in Toronto, slaving away as a tailor and seamstress to raise four children. Ida lived to see grandchildren and great-grandchildren, feeling immense pride when her descendants served in the IDF and attended her beloved Bais Yaakov in Israel.

The Ultimate Victory

"Not Hitler 'Yemach Shemo', not poverty, not adversity, not the loss of her Father... could ever or did ever alter her unwavering Emmunah (faith) in Hashem." - Rachel Bahar

"For whom I live and who do I have?"

Ida answered her own agonizing question from the ruins of 1945 by building a thriving family. Her archive is a testament to the power of the human spirit, the necessity of memory, and the indestructible nature of faith.
